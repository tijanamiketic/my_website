The last six weeks have brought two things at once. New models are arriving faster than ever, and labs are starting to publish claims of real progress in mathematics, computer science, physics and biology. Not every claim has been verified yet, but the pattern is hard to ignore.

## The pace of model releases

In this period, Google alone shipped three models from the Gemini 4 family: **Argon** for deep reasoning, **Carbon** for cybersecurity and long tasks, and **Hydrogen** as the fast model for everyday work. On November 9, Anthropic released **Claude Fable 5.2**. A new generation of models now arrives every few weeks, not every few months.

## Claims that could change science

**OpenAI: three discoveries at once.** On October 27, OpenAI announced that its internal models had reached results in three fields:

- **mathematics:** a proof in harmonic analysis, connected to the Kakeya conjecture;
- **computer science:** a result that OpenAI claims **refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis** (ETH), one of the basic assumptions about how hard some problems are for computers. The proof has been checked in the Lean system, and the review is led by a group of well-known complexity theorists;
- **physics:** mathematically rigorous existence proofs for some quantum field theories, including quantum chromodynamics.

On November 9, a panel led by Nobel laureate David Gross, chosen by OpenAI itself, confirmed the computer-verified version of the proof for Yang–Mills theory. The 1,147-page written proof has not been published yet.

**Anthropic: Fermat in 30 hours.** A team of 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents spent about 30 hours translating the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem into a form a computer can check. The first such effort, in September, took 11 days.

**Google DeepMind: AlphaGenome 2.** On November 11, DeepMind introduced a model that predicts how changes in DNA affect gene activity in **specific cell types and states**. On one immune cell dataset, it found almost twice as many causal variants as the previous version (30% versus 16%). That gives researchers a shorter path from a genetic link to a disease to its concrete cause.

## What's genuinely new here

First, **the curve of progress is still steep**. Every few weeks brings a measurable gain in speed, reliability or specialized capabilities.

Second, **the line between an "impressive demo" and a "useful scientific tool" is getting thinner**. Models no longer just answer questions. They work for long stretches on their own, check their own proofs and explore open problems.

## Caution is still warranted

Most of these results were published by the companies themselves, and some of the material isn't publicly available yet. In OpenAI's case, even the review panel was chosen by the company. That's why the growing use of systems like **Lean**, where a computer checks every step of a proof, is a good thing. Verifying results is becoming science's new bottleneck.

Whether every announced discovery will hold up to scrutiny remains to be seen. It's already clear, though, that the big AI labs see science and engineering as a key application, not a marketing trick. That shift could matter more than any single leaderboard result.
