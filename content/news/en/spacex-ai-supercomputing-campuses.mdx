SpaceX is known for rockets, but in recent weeks it has been making headlines for a completely different reason: the company is planning two huge AI supercomputing campuses, one in Texas and one in Arizona. The plans didn't come out in one big announcement. They surfaced gradually, through local permits, public notices of hearings and meetings with residents.

## Starbase, Texas: a supercomputer with its own power plant

According to a public notice from the city of Starbase, SpaceX wants to build, own and operate a **gas-fired power plant and a supercomputing center** on about 444 acres of land. The first phase is estimated at **about $24 billion**. Back on October 7, the city approved a 45-year development agreement with SpaceX, and job listings already mention a "Starbase supercomputer."

The key detail: the company isn't waiting for the power grid; it is generating its own electricity.

## Cochise County, Arizona: up to 6 gigawatts

In Arizona, the application was filed by a company called Tombstone Resources, and on November 4 SpaceX held a meeting with residents that drew a large crowd. The plan is big:

- **747 acres of land and nine buildings**, built in four phases of about 1.5 GW each;
- up to **6 gigawatts** of power use at full build-out. According to an analysis by _The Arizona Republic_, that's about 2.5 times more than all the data centers in Maricopa County (Phoenix) combined;
- about $3.6 billion for power grid upgrades, paid for by the developer.

The **$50 billion** figure mentioned for this project came from a local county official in a radio interview. SpaceX has not officially confirmed it. That's why the "over $70 billion" total for both campuses should be treated as an estimate, not an official announcement.

For both Arizona and Starbase, the first real test is **December 9**. That day, Cochise County holds a public hearing on the permit, and Starbase decides on tax incentives.

## Why SpaceX is doing this

SpaceX's AI business now operates under the name **SpaceXAI**, after xAI was merged into SpaceX. The company is already expanding its Colossus centers in Tennessee and Mississippi, and according to media reports the goal is about 13 GW of AI computing capacity by the end of 2027.

## The bigger picture

What stands out most in this story is vertical integration. Few players in the AI industry control land, power and computers all at once. It shows how **energy and real estate have become central to the AI race**. Whoever can secure gigawatts quickly and build capacity will have a lasting advantage.

There are also open questions that shouldn't be skipped: water and electricity use, the impact of a gas power plant and the views of local communities. That's exactly why these projects aren't decided by the company alone, but also by counties and cities.

The lesson: the next phase of AI progress depends on physical infrastructure as much as on model architecture. SpaceX is approaching computing power the same way it once approached rockets: build it yourself, at scale, and move fast.
